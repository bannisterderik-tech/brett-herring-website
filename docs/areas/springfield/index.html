Springfield, Oregon · Priority Area No. 3

Springfield: a working town with a river through it

Springfield started with a spring in a field on the Briggs family claim in 1848, got its mill race hand-dug in 1852, and spent the next century as a timber town. Booth-Kelly bought the sawmill in 1901, and by the 1907 city directory most of the town worked there. That history is not decoration here. It shaped the street grid, the housing stock, and a practical, unpretentious civic personality that people either get immediately or never quite do.

The property picture

What you will actually find.

Springfield's housing stock is older than the county average, with roughly six in ten homes built before 1980 and the 1970s the single biggest decade. Practically, that means solid bones and a wide range of condition. The east side toward Thurston runs newer and higher on ownership and value; the older core near downtown has the historic homes and the bigger projects. There is genuine range here, which is why first-time buyers keep finding their way in.

Living here

Willamalane runs a parks system most towns this size do not get. Dorris Ranch, 268 acres of working filbert orchard on the National Register, sits at the south edge of downtown. Clearwater Park puts a boat ramp and a disc golf course on the Middle Fork. The Middle Fork Path runs four miles between them and links to the three-mile Mill Race Path to make an eight-mile loop through town. The Christmas parade down Mohawk has been running for seven decades.

Schools

Springfield Public Schools serves the city, with Springfield High School and Thurston High School as the comprehensive high schools, plus the Academy of Arts and Academics downtown. Lane Community College is right here as well.

Local knowledge

Things you learn by living here.

  • The nickname before "Springtucky" was "Mill City," and it came from those old city directories where nearly everyone worked at Booth-Kelly.
  • The mill race is three and a half miles long and was dug by hand starting in 1852. It is the reason the town exists and it still runs past the old Booth-Kelly site.
  • Springfield's Simpsons connection is officially licensed, not a local legend. Matt Groening confirmed this is the Springfield, and the mural downtown at Fifth and Main was unveiled in 2014 as a partnership between Groening, Fox, and the city.
  • More than half of the commercial filbert trees growing in the United States trace back to nursery stock from Dorris Ranch, and the Willamette Valley grows the overwhelming majority of the country's hazelnuts.
  • Ken Kesey graduated from Springfield High. Clint Eastwood worked here as a log bronc operator for Weyerhaeuser.
  • The Springfield Museum building was a 1908 power substation that likely first electrified the Booth-Kelly mills.
