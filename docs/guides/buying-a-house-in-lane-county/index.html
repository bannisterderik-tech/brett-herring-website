How to Buy a House in Lane County, Oregon

The whole process from first lender call to keys in hand, in the order it actually happens, with the Lane County specifics nobody tells you.

Step one is a lender, not a listing

Everyone starts by scrolling houses. It is the fun part. But the lender conversation is what tells you which houses are real for you, and it is free. Twenty minutes on the phone gets you a number, a monthly payment estimate that includes taxes and insurance, and a list of any credit items worth fixing first. Do this before you tour anything, because falling for a house you cannot buy is a bad afternoon.

Get clear on the area before the house

Lane County covers everything from a walkable Eugene neighborhood to forty acres on a gravel road. The house is changeable. The location is not. Spend a Saturday driving the areas you are considering, at the time of day you would actually be living there. A street at 8am and the same street at 8pm can be two different answers.

Tour with a plan

Three to five well-chosen homes in an afternoon beats twelve. After the fourth house they all blur together anyway. Take notes on your phone. Photograph anything that concerns you. And pay attention to the things you cannot change: the lot, the light, the noise, the neighbors, the commute.

Writing an offer that gets taken seriously

Price matters, but so do closing timeline, earnest money, how you handle the appraisal, and whether your lender has a reputation for closing on time. In a competitive situation, a clean, well-structured offer from a buyer the seller believes in can beat a higher one from a buyer who looks shaky. That credibility is built on purpose.

The inspection is not a formality

Get one. Every time. In a hot market people talk about waiving inspections, and it is a genuinely bad idea for most buyers. Beyond the standard home inspection, consider a sewer scope on older homes, and on rural property add well and septic. A clean report gives you confidence. A rough one gives you the chance to renegotiate or walk before it is your problem.

Appraisal, underwriting, and the quiet weeks

After inspection comes the part where nothing appears to happen. Your lender orders the appraisal, underwriting asks for documents you already sent, and you wait. Do not open new credit accounts, do not change jobs if you can help it, and do not buy furniture on a card. Any of those can genuinely blow up your loan days before closing.

Closing and the first week

You will do a final walkthrough to confirm the house is in the condition you agreed to, sign a stack of documents, and get keys once the transaction records. Then change the locks, find the water shutoff, locate the electrical panel, and put a furnace filter on the calendar. Small things, but the people who do them in week one are the people who are not scrambling in month six.

FAQ

Questions this raises

How long does buying a house take in Lane County?

Once you have an accepted offer, typically thirty to forty-five days depending on your loan type. Finding the house varies far more. Prepared buyers often find it in a few weekends.

How much money do I need saved to buy?

You need a down payment, closing costs, and ideally a reserve for the first repairs. The down payment number is usually smaller than people assume, and Oregon assistance programs may help with it. Ask a lender for your real numbers.

Should I waive the inspection to win a bidding war?

Almost never worth it. There are better ways to strengthen an offer, including shortening the inspection period, adjusting the closing timeline, or increasing earnest money. Losing a house is recoverable. Buying one with a failed foundation is a different kind of problem.

What is earnest money and do I get it back?

It is a good faith deposit that shows the seller you are serious, and it credits toward your purchase at closing. If you cancel within the protections written into your contract, it is generally refundable. If you walk for reasons outside those protections, it may not be. Read that section carefully.
