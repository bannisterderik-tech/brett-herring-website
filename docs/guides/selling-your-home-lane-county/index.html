Selling Your Home in Lane County: The Prep-First Playbook

What to fix, what to skip, how pricing actually works, and why the first ten days on the market decide most of what happens after.

Start ninety days out if you can

The best listings are prepared, not rushed. Ninety days gives you time to handle repairs at normal prices instead of emergency prices, gather your paperwork, and schedule photography for a week the yard looks good. If you have got thirty days, we work with thirty days. But if you have got the runway, use it.

Fix the fear items, skip the vanity projects

Buyers discount hard for uncertainty. A roof at the end of its life, an ancient water heater, an electrical panel with a bad reputation, a crawlspace nobody has opened in a decade. Those cost you real money in negotiation. Meanwhile a full kitchen remodel rarely returns what you put in. Paint, lighting, hardware, landscaping, and a deep clean punch far above their weight.

Pricing is a strategy decision

The list price decides who shows up in the first ten days, which is when your listing gets the most attention it will ever get. Priced right, you create competition. Priced high with the idea of coming down later, you mostly generate a price reduction, a longer time on market, and a weaker position when an offer finally comes.

Photography is the actual first showing

Almost every buyer sees your house on a screen before they see it in person, and they decide in about four seconds whether to keep scrolling. That means real photography, shot at the right time of day, with the house genuinely ready. It is not the place to save money.

Get your paperwork together early

Permits for any work done, warranties, service records, and for rural property the well log, water test, and septic records. Sellers who can answer questions with documents keep control of the negotiation. Sellers who cannot are negotiating against a buyer's imagination.

The inspection period

Most deals that fall apart fall apart here. If you did the prep work, this is a formality with a short repair list. If you did not, you are reading a twenty page report alongside a buyer who now has leverage. This is the single strongest argument for doing the work before listing rather than after an offer.

Disclosure is not optional

Oregon sellers complete a property disclosure statement covering what you know about the condition of the home. Answer honestly. Trying to paper over a known problem is how a closed sale turns into a legal problem later. Disclosed issues are negotiable. Hidden ones are liabilities.

FAQ

Questions this raises

What should I fix before selling?

Prioritize anything a buyer would call a risk: roof, water heater, electrical, plumbing leaks, drainage. Then cosmetic items with high return like paint and lighting. Skip major remodels.

Is it worth staging my house?

For a vacant home, often yes, at least partially. For an occupied home, decluttering and depersonalizing usually gets most of the benefit for a fraction of the cost.

How long will it take to sell?

It depends on price, condition, and area. A well-prepared, correctly priced home in a desirable Lane County area moves quickly. An overpriced one can sit for months regardless of how nice it is.

Do I have to disclose problems with my house?

Yes. Oregon requires a seller property disclosure statement and honesty about known material issues. Disclosure protects you. Concealment creates liability that outlives the sale.
