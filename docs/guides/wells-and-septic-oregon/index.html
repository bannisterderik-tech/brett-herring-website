Wells and Septic Systems: A Plain-English Guide for Lane County

How wells and septic systems actually work, what to inspect, what maintenance costs, and the questions to ask before you buy a property on either.

Owning your water

A private well is a pump, a pressure tank, and a hole in the ground reaching an aquifer. When it works, you forget it exists. When it does not, you have no water at all, which is a genuinely different experience from a city outage. Understanding your system before you own it is the whole game.

Reading a well log

Oregon keeps records of drilled wells. The log tells you when it was drilled, how deep, how it was constructed, and what flow rate the driller measured. That last number is a snapshot from drilling day, not a guarantee for today, which is why a current flow test during your inspection matters.

Flow rate in practical terms

Flow rate is how fast the well can deliver water. Lower flow does not automatically mean unlivable. It means you plan around it with storage tanks and by not running the sprinklers while somebody showers. What you want is to know the number before you own it rather than discover it in August.

Testing your water, and the Oregon rule most people miss

Oregon law requires that when a home served by a domestic well is sold, the seller has the well tested for arsenic, nitrates, and total coliform bacteria upon accepting an offer, and provides those results to both the buyer and the state health authority. That is a statutory obligation, not a courtesy, and it is one of the more useful consumer protections in Oregon real estate. It also does not cover everything, so depending on the area you may want additional testing. As an owner, retest periodically, especially after flooding or any work on the well.

How septic actually works

Waste flows to a tank where solids settle, then liquid moves out to a drainfield where soil does the final treatment. The tank needs periodic pumping. The drainfield needs to not be driven on, built over, or overloaded. Treated well, systems last a long time. Neglected, drainfield replacement is a serious expense.

What to check before you buy

Pull the county permit record. Get a septic inspection, ideally with the tank pumped so it can actually be examined. Confirm the permitted capacity, since it ties to bedroom count and limits future additions. Locate the drainfield and look at what is on top of it.

Living with both

Pump the tank on schedule. Do not put wipes, grease, or harsh chemicals into the system. Keep vehicles off the drainfield. Know where your well head is and keep it protected. It is a short list, and following it is the difference between routine maintenance and a five-figure surprise.

FAQ

Questions this raises

How often should a septic tank be pumped?

Commonly every few years, depending on tank size and household size. Your inspector or septic company will give you a schedule based on your specific system. Keep the receipts, because buyers ask for them.

What is a good well flow rate?

Higher is better, but the practical question is whether it supports your household with reasonable storage. A lower-producing well with a storage tank can serve a family fine. Get a current test and a professional opinion.

Does the seller have to test the well when selling a house in Oregon?

Yes. Oregon requires the seller of a home served by a domestic well to test for arsenic, nitrates, and total coliform bacteria once an offer is accepted, and to deliver those results to the buyer and to the state. Ask for them.

Can I get a mortgage on a house with a well and septic?

Yes. Lenders commonly want a satisfactory water test and sometimes a septic inspection. This is routine, not an obstacle.

What does it cost to replace a septic system?

It varies widely with system type, soil, and site. It is expensive enough that verifying the existing system before purchase is one of the highest-value inspections you can pay for.
