Buying Rural Property in Lane County: Wells, Septic, Zoning, Access

The four things that decide whether a piece of country property is a dream or an expensive lesson, and how to check all of them before you are committed.

The city rules stop at the city limits

In town, water and sewer are somebody else's problem and zoning mostly means you cannot open a bar in your garage. Out here, water comes from a well you own, waste goes to a septic system you maintain, and zoning determines whether you can build the shop you have been drawing on napkins. None of it is hard. All of it is expensive to discover late.

Water: the well

Ask for the well log, which records depth, construction, and the flow rate measured when it was drilled. Get a current flow test and a water quality test during your inspection period. Find out whether the well is shared, and if it is, read the shared well agreement. A modest producing well is workable with storage and planning. An unknown well is a gamble.

Waste: the septic system

Lane County holds septic permit records. You want the system type, the install date, the drainfield location, and the pumping and inspection history. Critically, you want the permitted capacity, because it is tied to bedroom count. If your plan is to add bedrooms later, the existing system may not support it, and that is a project with a real price tag.

Rules: zoning and what you can build

Oregon land use law is genuinely strict, and farm and forest zones exist to keep working land working. That means new dwellings, second homes for family, and some home businesses face real limits. If your purchase depends on being able to build something, verify it with county planning before you remove your contingencies. Get the answer in writing.

Access: how you legally get there

Driving down a road is not the same as having a legal right to use it. Look for recorded easements, road maintenance agreements, and any seasonal access limits. Shared gravel roads work fine when the agreement is clear and neighbors cooperate. They become a problem when nobody wrote anything down.

Financing and insurance

Not every lender is comfortable with acreage, outbuildings, or mixed use property. USDA loans may be available in eligible areas. On the insurance side, wildfire risk affects availability and price in parts of the county, and it is worth getting a quote early rather than assuming. Both of these are better solved before you are under contract.

What it is actually like to live out here

Longer drives for everything. Your own responsibility for water and waste. Weather that occasionally takes your power out. Also: quiet, room, sky, and neighbors who show up with a tractor when you need one. It is a genuinely different life, and the people who love it really love it.

FAQ

Questions this raises

What is the most common mistake buying rural property?

Falling for the view and skipping the diligence. Water, septic, zoning, and legal access are the four items that decide whether the property works. Check all four before your contingencies expire.

How do I find the well log for a property?

Oregon maintains well log records through the Water Resources Department. Your agent or the seller should be able to produce it. If nobody can find one, that is worth knowing too.

Can I put a shop or barn on farm-zoned land?

Often yes for genuine farm use, with permits, but the rules are specific and vary by zone. Confirm with Lane County planning before you buy counting on it.

Is well water safe to drink?

Frequently yes, but you test rather than assume. A standard test covers bacteria and nitrates, and depending on the area you may want additional testing. Then you retest periodically as an owner.
